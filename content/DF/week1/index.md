#### Introduction to Digital Forensics and Legal Systems

+ Process that consist of:
  + Identifying where evidence resides.
  + Collecting it without altering it.
  + Preserving its integrity.
  + Analysing for patterns or proof.
  + Presenting it clearly in court.
+ Every step must meet legal standards, or the evidence may be thrown away.
+ Forensic investigators and courts must carefully balance law enforcement’s need for evidence with individuals’ rights.
+ Jurisdictional Challenges
  + Cybercrimes often span multiple countries
  + Each country has different laws on privacy and data access
  + Treaties are slow and often clash with national privacy rules
  + Capturing network traffic requires careful choices about scope, precise timestamping to accurately reconstruct events, and a clear chain of custody to ensure the evidence is reliable and legally admissible.
+ Investigators must balance the need for security and evidence with privacy concerns, while also navigating the legal frameworks that govern decryption and data access.


#### Information Security in the Modern Enterprise

+ The best tools are useless unless applied intelligently to match a dynamic threat landscape
+ The information security function is more central to preserving corporate value than ever
+ In the world of intellectual property, there are three areas that matter for data:
  + The simplest is copyright, which protects only the specific expression of an idea, not the idea itself
  + Patents protect novel inventions
  + trade secrets protect any information that is not generally known and that provides some competitive advantage
+ In order to have access to these legal remedies, a company must first have complied with a basic requirement: it must show that it exercised “reasonable efforts” to prevent the loss
+ Challenges facing information security:
  + everyone else has very high expectations for data security
  + modern threats are ubiquitous and dynamic
  + corporate systems were designed for performance, not for security
  + systems have been fractured, making them much harder to control
  + the modern corporation is often ambivalent about secrecy
  + the general attitude about security within the organization
+ information security depends heavily on classical risk assessment
  + and risk management is closely related to business continuity
+ Awareness and ownership have to happen at the board level
+ Know Where to Begin
  + first operational step is to conduct an assessment of the organization's existing efforts to protect the integrity of its data
  + we need to look outside for independent advice
  + for smaller companies it's not always necessary to spend what it takes for that kind of service
    + often better to start with a free external reference source that can guide you through a do-it-yourself process
+ Risk Management
  + The basic notion is that any organization should be able to identify a set of events that might happen to imperil their business
    + managers can then take informed decisions about whether to ignore the risk, accept it as is, transfer it to some other entity, or mitigate the risk
+ Public Standards for Information Security
  + the leading example of risk management applied to information security is the ISO/IEC 27001 standard
    + sets very specific requirements for security management systems and controls,
  + another very useful construct for addressing data security is provided by the “NIST Framework,”
    + treats issues well beyond the IT and physical security environment, including management and governance, staff policies and procedures, training, and supply chain management
+ Creating the Security Plan
  + requires a comprehensive focus on all the ways in which the company functions and internally communicates
  + Information Protection Team: it should constitute itself as an ongoing cross-departmental structure, meeting periodically to check in and assess progress
  + Process:
    + Identify
      + Asset management
      + Business environment
      + Governance
    + Protect
      + Access control
      + Awareness and training
      + Protective technology
    + Detect
      + Anomaly and events
      + Security continuous monitoring
      + Detection processes
    + Respond
      + Response planning
      + Analysis and mitigations
      + Improvement
    + Recover
      + Recovery planning
      + Improvements
      + Communications
  + Management of third parties
    + actively manage how those third parties deal with their own systems
  + Security and Confidentiality Management
    + Managers in all areas of the company should project and demonstrate their concern by carefully following procedures and insisting on the same from those whom they manage
  + Monitoring and Measurement
    + observing how well the overall security processes are actually working
  + Corrective Actions and Improvements
    + be aware of how you have been doing and to what extent you might be off course
    + be prepared to make changes to fix whatever is not working and return the system to optimum performance
+ Policies and Procedures
  + There is no “one size fits all” approach to establishing security policies and procedures
  + consider how valuable the information is, the nature of the threat of loss that it faces, and whether reducing the risk is worth the cost of specific protective measures
  + An Agenda for Action for Basic Protections That you Should Always Consider
    + Premises security:
      + Visitors should be required to sign in
      + keep particularly sensitive areas off limits
      + smartphone cameras can be used to capture valuable information
    + Classification of data
      +  Sensitive information in document should be classified and clearly marked as confidentia
      + Paper documents should be controlled to limit copying and to provide for secure destruction
    + Employee-owned devices
      + provisions that address privacy rights and the ability to access and remove company data
    + Technological protections
      + Deploy appropriate technology to help prevent network breaches
      +  Reserve sufficient attention and resources for monitoring and detection systems
    + Policies and rules
      + publish clear policies and rules around information security hygiene
      + Monitor for compliance, and to the extent it falls short, reconsider either the rules, your training or your enforcement
    + Contracts
      + Employees and consultants should be required to sign legally binding confidentiality agreements
    + Management
      + Establish clear points of authority and accountability
    + Education
      + proper training of the workforce is the most cost-effective step that can be taken to improve information security
+ Training
  + one aspect of information security that has remained constant over the decades is that humans are the biggest single source of loss
  + means that behaviors can be changed, through effective education and training
  + training should be inclusive
    + not be limited to new hires, or to the people who have access to the most sensitive information
    + everyone in the company should have a grounded understanding of the importance of data security
  + the training should be interesting and fresh
    + keep changing how and where it's done, what people are presenting, what media are used, and what issues are addressed
    + Try to make it memorable
  + should be continuous, not just an occasional class or webinar
    + do not give in to the impulse to first cut the training budget, at least not for information security

##### First Post

+ Why is digital evidence often considered more fragile than traditional forms of evidence (e.g., physical objects or written records)?
  + Digital forensics has different challenges; two main ones are volatile data, anti-forensic techniquesc and data distribution. First, some of the data on a digital system is volatile, like memory and temporary files. This can be deleted, modified, or created at any time when interacting with the system. For instance, if taking a memory snapshot, this will change before, during, and after the process. This makes digital evidence fragile to begin with, as this evidence can change without an adversary attempting to change it (McDown et al. 2016). Second, anti-forensic techniques can be difficult to detect once implemented and can even completely destroy critical evidence to completely understand the evidence. In cases like ransomware, where criminals delete all logs and systems do not upload the logs to a SIEM or similar system, they leave almost no trace, which disrupts a forensic investigation (Surakanti, et al. 2025). Finally, data can be distributed in different parts of the world. For instance an adversary in Affrica can have their proxy server in USA, their command and control server on Russia, attacking a business in Europe. This shows how data can be in multiple parts of the world and can be difficult to trace because of legal, privacy or international agreements (Lin, 2016).


#### Seminar
+ How could AI support, but not replace, forensic expertise?
  + AI excels at processing vast datasets to provide rapid event summaries, identify anomalies, or suggest high-level investigative leads. However, these technologies have significant limitations, like the risk of "hallucinations," where the model generates plausible but entirely false information. Such errors could derail an investigation or lead to flawed conclusions. Consequently, human experts remain indispensable; they provide the necessary oversight, contextual corrections, and validation to ensure the AI’s output is accurate and legally sound.

+ What are the ethical and evidential risks of using AI tools in legal contexts?
  + The use of AI in legal frameworks introduces several critical risks:
    + Data Privacy: AI models often log input data for training or telemetry, which can lead to severe GDPR or confidentiality violations depending on the model's hosting environment.
    + Accuracy: The potential for incorrect or biased outputs requires rigorous expert verification before any information is submitted to a case.
    + The "Black Box" Problem: AI often fails to provide a transparent rationale for its conclusions. This lack of explainability undermines the reproducibility of results, which is a fundamental requirement for evidence in a court of law.

+ Should AI-generated findings ever be admissible as evidence? Why or why not?
  + AI-generated findings should never be admissible without direct expert supervision and validation. While AI is a powerful tool for discovery, evidence must meet strict standards of reliability, transparency, and reproducibility. Because many AI models cannot currently explain their "reasoning" or guarantee consistent results under the same conditions, their findings should only serve as a supportive resource for a human expert rather than stand-alone evidence.

#### Reflexion

+ Digital forensics acquisition is fraught with challenges, ranging from sophisticated anti-forensic techniques to complex legal and privacy considerations. These considerations often vary significantly depending on the jurisdiction and the specific laws of the country involved.

Despite these hurdles, perhaps the most fundamental challenge is the availability of evidence itself. In many instances, administrators fail to enable or preserve critical system logs. When this lack of logging is coupled with an adversary’s deliberate efforts to delete existing records, investigators are left with a vacuum of information, making a comprehensive forensic reconstruction nearly impossible.
